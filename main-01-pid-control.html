PID Control — Expanded

01 Why This Matters Now
⚡ Hook Every process loop you touch — temperature, pressure, flow, level — is controlled by a PID. If you can't read what the three terms are doing, you're flying blind when the loop starts hunting, oscillating, or drifting off setpoint. Now that you understand DC circuits, wiring, diagrams, cabinets, and signal flow, you have the foundation to understand PID at the depth it deserves.

Proportional Action — Deep Dive

What it does: The proportional term produces an output that is directly proportional to the current error. If error doubles, the proportional contribution doubles. If error is zero, the proportional contribution is zero.

Poutput = Kp × e(t)

Where:

  • Kp = proportional gain. Higher gain = stronger response to a given error.
  • e(t) = error at the current moment = SP − PV.
  • Poutput = the contribution of the P term to the final controller output.

Proportional Band — The Concept You'll See in the Field

In many DCS and PLC platforms, P action is not expressed as Kp but as Proportional Band (PB) — the percentage of the measurement range that causes the output to go from 0% to 100%.

PB = 100 / Kp
KpProportional BandEffect
0.5200%Very low gain — slow, sluggish response
1.0100%Moderate — full output range for full error range
2.050%Higher gain — faster but more oscillatory
5.020%Very high gain — likely unstable, oscillating
10.010%Extreme — almost certainly unstable
⚠ Critical A controller with P action only will always have steady-state offset. If the error goes to zero, the P output goes to zero — and the valve closes. But then error comes back because the valve is closed. The loop settles at a nonzero error where the P output exactly balances the load. This is called droop or offset, and it's why we need integral action.

Integral (Reset) Action — Deep Dive

What it does: The integral term produces an output that is proportional to the accumulated error over time. Even a small error, if it persists, will eventually drive the integral contribution higher and higher until the error is eliminated.

Ioutput = Ki × ∫ e(t) dt

Where:

  • Ki = integral gain. Higher Ki = faster integral action.
  • ∫ e(t) dt = the running sum of error over time (the area under the error curve).
  • Ioutput = the contribution of the I term to the final output.

Integral Time — The Other Way You'll See It

Many platforms express integral action as Integral Time (Ti) or Reset Time — the time it takes for the integral term to produce the same output as the proportional term did for a given step error.

Ti = 1 / Ki

The crucial inversion:

  • Higher Ki = more integral action = faster correction, more overshoot.
  • Higher Ti = less integral action = slower correction, more stable.
  • If you're tuning in a platform that uses Ti, increasing Ti makes the loop more sluggish. This is the opposite of what most people expect.

Integral Windup — The #1 Field Problem

When the controller output saturates — valve fully open, motor at max speed — and the error persists, the integral term keeps accumulating. The controller "winds up" to an extreme output value. When the process finally responds and the error reverses, the integral term has to "unwind" from that extreme value. During this unwind, the controller output is stuck at saturation, and the process overshoots badly before the controller can recover.

Integral windup: during saturation, the I term keeps accumulating, causing overshoot when the process finally responds.

Anti-windup strategies:

  • Integral clamp: Limits the maximum value the integral term can reach. Simple and effective.
  • Back-calculation: Feeds the saturation error back to reduce the integral term. Used by many DCS platforms.
  • Conditional integration: Stops integrating when output is saturated. The most aggressive form.
  • Bumpless transfer: Ensures the integral term is initialized to match the output when switching from manual to auto. Prevents sudden jumps.

Derivative (Rate) Action — Deep Dive

What it does: The derivative term produces an output proportional to the rate of change of the error. It acts as a brake — when error is decreasing rapidly, D counteracts the P output to prevent overshoot.

Doutput = Kd × de(t)/dt

Where:

  • Kd = derivative gain. Higher Kd = stronger braking action.
  • de(t)/dt = the rate of change of error (the slope of the error curve).
  • Doutput = the contribution of the D term to the final output.

Derivative Time (Td)

Most platforms express derivative action as Derivative Time (Td) — the time by which the D term "anticipates" the future error.

Td = Kd / Kp

Why D Is Often Left at Zero

  • Noise amplification: Derivative reacts to rate of change. Noise is rapid change. So D amplifies noise like crazy — the output becomes jittery and the valve hunts constantly.
  • Most loops don't need it: For temperature, level, and slow flow loops, P and I are enough. D adds complexity without much benefit.
  • D is most useful on fast loops: Pressure, fast flow, and motion control benefit from D. Temperature loops — where thermal lag is huge — usually don't.
  • Never use D on noisy signals without first filtering the PV. Even then, most technicians leave D at zero unless there's a specific reason to use it.
⚠ Field Rule If you don't know why you're adding D, don't add D. Nine times out of ten, it makes things worse. The exception is when you have a specific overshoot problem that P and I can't solve — and even then, use it sparingly.

The Complete PID Equation

Output(t) = Kp·e(t) + Ki·∫e(t)dt + Kd·(de/dt)

Or in the parallel form with Ti and Td:

Output(t) = Kp · [ e(t) + (1/Ti)·∫e(t)dt + Td·(de/dt) ]

P, I, D — What Each One Actually Does to the Loop

TermResponds ToEffect of IncreasingEffect of Decreasing

IAccumulated error over timeEliminates offset faster, more overshoot, can cause instabilitySlower offset elimination, more stable, may never eliminate offset

04 Tuning Methods

How to Actually Set the Numbers

Knowing what P, I, and D do is step one. Knowing how to set them is step two. There are several methods, ranging from mathematical to pure trial-and-error.

Method 1: Ziegler-Nichols (Closed-Loop / Oscillation Method)

The most famous tuning method. It's aggressive, produces fast response, and is a great starting point — but often requires fine-tuning for real loops.

  • Set Ki = 0 and Kd = 0. You want only proportional action.
  • Put the loop in automatic mode with a small setpoint change.
  • Increase Kp slowly until the loop just begins to oscillate continuously. The oscillation should be sustained — neither growing nor decaying.
  • Record Ku — the ultimate gain. This is the Kp value that produced sustained oscillation.
  • Record Pu — the ultimate period. This is the time (in seconds or minutes) for one complete oscillation cycle.
  • Calculate the tuning parameters from the table below.
Controller TypeKpTi (Integral Time)Td (Derivative Time)
P only0.5 · Ku——
PI0.45 · KuPu / 1.2—
PID0.6 · KuPu / 2Pu / 8
✓ Practical Note Ziegler-Nichols produces aggressive tuning — fast response but often too much overshoot for real process loops. Many technicians use it as a starting point and then reduce Kp by 20-30% and increase Ti by 20-30% for a more conservative, stable loop.

Method 2: Ziegler-Nichols (Open-Loop / Reaction Curve Method)

This method doesn't require the loop to oscillate. Instead, you perform a step test and measure the process reaction curve.

  • Put the controller in manual.
  • Step the output by a known amount (e.g., 5%).
  • Record the PV response over time.
  • Draw the steepest tangent line to the response curve.
  • Measure: L (dead time — delay before the PV responds) and T (time constant — time for PV to reach 63% of its final change).
  • Calculate the tuning parameters:
Controller TypeKpTiTd
P onlyT / L——
PI0.9 · T / L3.3 · L—
PID1.2 · T / L2 · L0.5 · L

Method 3: Trial and Error (Field Method)

The method most technicians actually use. It's slower but safer — you never let the loop go unstable.

  • Start with Ki = 0, Kd = 0. Tune P first.
  • Increase Kp in small steps until the loop responds quickly but is still stable. Back off 20%.
  • Add integral action. Start with a long Ti (slow integral) and gradually shorten it (more aggressive) until offset is eliminated without overshoot.
  • Add derivative only if needed. If the loop still overshoots, add a small amount of D (short Td). Increase carefully, watching for noise.
  • Test with a setpoint change. The PV should reach SP with minimal overshoot and settle quickly.
  • Test with a load change. Introduce a disturbance (open a bypass, change feed rate) and observe recovery.
  • Write down the final values and document the tuning.
✓ Field Tuning Rules One change at a time. Never adjust two parameters simultaneously — you won't know which one caused the change. Make small steps. A 10-20% change in a parameter is usually enough. Document every change. Write down the before and after values, and the observed effect.
05 Troubleshooting — Why Loops Oscillate

Multiple Reasons for Oscillation

Oscillation is the most common PID problem in the field. It has many causes, and the fix depends on which one is actually happening. Here are the most common — ordered by frequency in the field.

CauseSignatureFix
Kp too highFast oscillation, roughly sine-wave, period proportional to loop dynamicsReduce Kp (increase PB) by 20-30%
Ki too high (or Ti too short)Slow oscillation, larger amplitude, overshoot after disturbancesReduce Ki (increase Ti)
Kd too highHigh-frequency jitter, valve hunting constantlyReduce or eliminate Kd; check PV filtering
Integral windupOvershoot after long saturation, slow recoveryEnable anti-windup; check saturation limits
Valve hysteresis / stictionLimit cycle — small sustained oscillation, valve never quite settlesRepair or replace valve; add valve positioner; reduce PB
Process dead time too longOscillation regardless of tuning — loop fundamentally can't be tuned tighterReduce dead time (move sensor, reduce pipe volume); accept slower tuning
Two controllers fightingCascade or interacting loops oscillate out of phaseDecouple loops; tune inner loop first; check for hidden interactions
Noise on PV signalHigh-frequency jitter, especially with D actionFilter PV; shield cable; separate from power wiring; add signal isolator
✓ Diagnostic Trick Oscillation frequency tells you the cause. Fast oscillation (period of seconds) usually points to tuning (Kp too high) or noise. Slow oscillation (period of minutes) usually points to integral action or process dead time. A valve that never quite settles often points to mechanical hysteresis.
06 Troubleshooting — Why Loops Fail to Reach Setpoint

Multiple Reasons for Failure to Reach SP

A loop that never reaches setpoint (and isn't oscillating) has a different set of causes. The PV sits below SP (or above) and stays there.

CauseSignatureFix
Integral action is offPermanent offset, loop is stable but PV is below/above SPTurn on integral action; reduce Ti (increase Ki)
Ki too low (Ti too long)Very slow correction toward SP, may never arriveIncrease Ki / reduce Ti
Output saturatedValve is fully open (or fully closed) and PV is still below SPCheck valve sizing, process capacity, upstream supply; loop may be undersized
Valve undersizedValve at 100% and PV still can't reach SP at full demandReplace with larger valve; check Cv rating
Valve stuck / failedOutput signal changes but valve position doesn't moveCheck valve actuator, air supply, positioner
Transmitter calibration errorPV reads low (or high) consistently, doesn't match a test instrumentRecalibrate transmitter
Setpoint is unrealisticSP is beyond the process capability — physically impossibleVerify SP against process design; reset to achievable value
Load exceeds capacityProcess demand is greater than what the loop can deliverAdd capacity, reduce load, or accept lower SP
Manual/auto mismatchController is in manual; output not tracking SPSwitch to auto; check tracking settings
Cascade slave in wrong modeInner loop not following outer loop's setpointPut slave in cascade mode; verify cascade initialization
✓ Diagnostic Trick Check the output. If the output is at 100% (or 0%), the loop is saturated — it's not a tuning problem, it's a capacity problem. If the output is somewhere in the middle and stable, the loop is either missing integral action or has a calibration error. If the output is changing but the PV isn't, the problem is downstream — valve, actuator, or process.
07 Hands-On Exercise

Simulate, Tune, Troubleshoot

Exercise A: PID Simulator — Full Tuning Session

Go to pidtuner.com or search "PID simulator online." Run through this complete tuning session:

  • Baseline: Set Kp=1, Ki=0, Kd=0. Apply a setpoint step. Observe the steady-state offset.
  • Tune P: Increase Kp until the loop responds quickly but doesn't oscillate. Note the value. This is your "P only" tuning.
  • Add I: Start with a small Ki (0.01). Increase slowly until the offset is eliminated. Watch for overshoot. Find the Ki that eliminates offset with minimal overshoot.
  • Cause windup: Set Ki=5 (way too high). Apply a large setpoint step. Watch the windup and overshoot. Reset Ki to a reasonable value.
  • Add D: If there's still overshoot, add a small amount of Kd (0.1). Increase carefully, watching for noise. Find the Kd that smooths the approach.
  • Test disturbance rejection: With your tuned values, introduce a load disturbance (if the simulator supports it). Watch the recovery.
  • Document your tuning: Write down the final Kp, Ki, Kd, and describe the response.

Exercise B: Ziegler-Nichols Tuning

Using the same simulator, perform the Ziegler-Nichols closed-loop method:

  • Set Ki=0, Kd=0.
  • Increase Kp until the loop oscillates with constant amplitude. Record Ku and Pu.
  • Calculate Kp, Ti, Td from the Z-N table.
  • Enter those values. Observe the response. Is it faster than your manual tuning? Is it more oscillatory?
  • Apply the "practical correction" — reduce Kp by 25%, increase Ti by 25%. Compare again.

Exercise C: Troubleshooting Scenarios

For each scenario, identify the most likely cause and the fix:

  • Scenario 1: A flow loop oscillates with a period of about 3 seconds. Amplitude is steady. The valve is moving constantly. The PV signal looks clean. (Answer: Kp too high — reduce Kp.)
  • Scenario 2: A temperature loop oscillates slowly, period about 5 minutes. Overshoots by 15°F, then undershoots by 10°F, slowly decaying. (Answer: Ki too high or Ti too short — reduce integral action.)
  • Scenario 3: A pressure loop sits at 2 PSI below setpoint indefinitely. No oscillation, just steady offset. Output is at 45%. (Answer: Integral action off or Ki too low — enable or increase integral.)
  • Scenario 4: A level loop's valve signal is at 100% but the level won't reach setpoint. The valve is fully open. (Answer: Valve undersized or process demand exceeds capacity — check valve Cv and upstream supply.)
  • Scenario 5: After a large setpoint change, the loop overshoots by 30% and takes a long time to recover. (Answer: Integral windup — check anti-windup settings or reduce Ki.)
  • Scenario 6: A flow loop oscillates with a very small amplitude at high frequency (2-3 Hz). The valve is jittering. D action is set to 0.5. (Answer: Kd too high, amplifying noise — reduce Kd or filter the PV.)
