Terminal Boards & Control Cabinets

01 Why This Matters Now
⚡ Hook A loop diagram tells you that wire 101-1 goes from FT-101 to TB1-1. But where is TB1-1 physically? Which cabinet? Which side? Which row? If you can't find the terminal in the panel, the drawing is useless. And when you're building or modifying a panel, you have to know where everything goes — not just electrically, but physically. Terminal blocks and cabinet layout are the bridge between the drawing and the real world.
02 The Analogy

A control cabinet is a city. Terminal blocks are its intersections.

In a city, every road, every block, every building has a purpose and a location. You don't put a hospital in the middle of a residential block. You don't put a highway interchange where a quiet street should be. Control cabinets work the same way — everything has a designated zone, and the terminal blocks are the intersections where different networks meet.

03 Core Concepts

1. What Terminal Blocks Actually Do

A terminal block is more than a place to screw wires down. It serves several critical functions:

  • Mechanical junction: It provides a reliable, reusable connection point between a field wire and a panel wire. Field wire comes in from the field; panel wire goes to the PLC. The terminal is where they meet.
  • Isolation point: You can disconnect a loop at the terminal for testing, without disturbing the field wiring or the panel wiring. This is why terminals matter for troubleshooting.
  • Standardization: Field wires land on the bottom of a terminal; panel wires land on the top. This standard means anyone opening the cabinet knows where to look.
  • Organization: Terminals group wires by loop, by signal type, or by destination. A well-organized terminal strip tells you the whole story of the panel just by looking at it.
  • Test point: Many terminals have built-in test points (a small hole in the top) where you can touch a meter probe without loosening the wire.

3. DIN Rail — The Mounting Standard

Almost all modern terminal blocks and panel devices mount on DIN rail — a standardized metal strip. This is what makes control panels modular and reconfigurable.

DIN Rail TypeDimensionsCommon Use
TS35 (top hat)35mm wide, 7.5mm or 15mm deepIndustry standard — most terminals, PLCs, relays
TS1515mm wideSmall terminals, tight spaces
TS32 (G-rail)32mm wide, asymmetricOlder European equipment

Key advantage: You can snap a terminal onto the rail, slide it to position, and lock it with an end stop. No tools required for mounting. This is why panel modification is fast and clean.

DIN rail is the universal mounting standard. Terminals, relays, PLCs, breakers, and power supplies all snap onto it.

4. Terminal Block Configurations

  • Single-level: One connection point per side. The basic 2-wire terminal. Most common for simple wiring.
  • Double-level (2-tier): Two connections stacked vertically. Doubles your wire density without needing a wider rail. Common in dense cabinets.
  • Triple-level (3-tier): Three levels. Used for 3-wire or 4-wire devices (like 3-wire RTDs or 4-20 mA with separate power).
  • Ground terminal (PE): Green/yellow colored. Connects directly to the DIN rail, which is grounded to the cabinet's earth bar. Every cabinet has these.
  • Fused terminal: Has a built-in fuse. Protects the panel wiring without needing a separate fuse holder.
  • Disconnect terminal: Has a lever or plug that opens the circuit without removing wires. Great for testing.
  • Test terminal: Has a test point (usually a small hole in the top) for meter probes. Doesn't require loosening the wire.

6. Numbering & Labeling — The Rules

Terminal numbering follows industry conventions that let anyone trace a wire from the drawing to the physical panel.

  • TB designation: Each terminal strip gets a name — TB1, TB2, TB3, etc. Larger strips get divided into sections (TB1A, TB1B).
  • Sequential numbering: Terminals are numbered 1, 2, 3, 4... left to right, top to bottom. This is the physical position on the strip.
  • Wire number ≠ terminal number: A wire labeled 101-1 lands on terminal 5. The wire has its own number, the terminal has its own number, and the drawing links them.
  • Labels are mandatory: Every terminal has a marker. Every wire has a label within 2 inches of the terminal. No exceptions.
  • Numbering convention groups: Terminals for analog inputs might be TB1-1 through TB1-20; digital inputs TB1-21 through TB1-60; outputs TB1-61 through TB1-100. This is called structured numbering and makes troubleshooting much faster.

7. Control Cabinet Layout — The Big Picture

A well-designed cabinet is organized into zones. Each zone has a purpose, and devices within a zone share similar characteristics. Here's the standard layout (from top to bottom):

Standard cabinet zone layout: power at top, logic in the middle, terminals below the logic, cable entry at the bottom, earth bar running the full height on the side.

8. Zone-by-Zone Layout Rules

Zone 1 — Incoming Power (top):

  • Main disconnect switch (lockable) at the top so it can be reached quickly in emergency.
  • Surge protection devices (SPDs) on the incoming feed.
  • Distribution breakers for downstream circuits.
  • Kept at the top because heat rises and power components generate the most heat.

Zone 2 — DC Power Supplies:

  • 24 VDC power supplies (usually redundant or in parallel for critical systems).
  • DC distribution breakers or fused terminal blocks.
  • Voltage monitoring relays or alarms.
  • Buffering capacitors if the loads have high inrush.

Zone 3 — Logic / Control:

  • PLC CPU, power supply module, I/O cards, communication cards.
  • Interposing relays for high-power outputs.
  • Signal conditioners (isolators, splitters, converters).
  • Located in the middle — easiest access for troubleshooting and firmware updates.

Zone 4 — Terminal Blocks:

  • All field wiring lands here. Bottom of the terminal = field wire; top = panel wire.
  • Analog signals on one strip, digital on another, power on another.
  • Ground terminals at the end of each strip, tied to the earth bar.
  • Below the logic so field wires have the shortest run up to the PLC cards.

Zone 5 — Cable Entry (bottom):

  • Cable glands that provide strain relief and environmental sealing.
  • Shield bars where analog cable shields are terminated (one end only).
  • Entry from below keeps water and dust from entering through the top.
  • Separate entries for power and signal cables to minimize EMI coupling.

9. Wire Duct and Routing

Wires are routed through wire duct (also called cable trunking or Panduit) — slotted plastic channels that keep wires organized and protected. Proper routing rules:

  • Power and signal separated: Never run 120 VAC power wires in the same duct as 24 VDC signal wires. EMI from the power wire couples into the signal.
  • Analog separated from digital: Digital switching noise can couple into analog signals. Use separate ducts or a partition.
  • Fill ratio: Don't fill duct more than 50-60%. The wires need room to lay flat, and tight bundling traps heat.
  • Vertical and horizontal trunks: Design the duct layout like a road system — major vertical trunks on the sides, horizontal branches to each device.
  • Cover after wiring: Duct covers go on after wiring is complete. They keep wires in place and protect them from accidental contact.

11. Heat Management

Electronics generate heat. Cabinets trap it. Overheating is the #1 cause of premature electronics failure.

  • Passive cooling: Louvered vents, natural convection. Works for low-power panels.
  • Filtered fan: Small fan + filter pulls cool air in and pushes hot air out. Most common for medium panels.
  • Air conditioner: Closed-loop cooling for high-power or outdoor cabinets. Required when ambient temperature exceeds component rating.
  • Heat exchanger: For hazardous-area cabinets — cools without opening the enclosure to outside air.
  • Rule of thumb: Interior temperature should be 10°C below the lowest-rated component in the panel. If any device is rated to 50°C, keep the panel under 40°C.

12. Grounding — The Silent Requirement

  • Earth bar: A copper bar running the height of the cabinet, connected to the plant's grounding grid. Every ground wire terminates here.
  • DIN rail grounding: The DIN rail is bonded to the earth bar via a ground clamp.
  • PE terminals: Green/yellow terminals on every terminal strip. Connected directly to the earth bar.
  • Shield bars: Separate from earth bar (or connected to it via a dedicated clamp). Analog cable shields terminate here.
  • Bonding jumpers: Bolted connections between the cabinet door, cabinet body, and backplate — ensures all metal parts are at the same potential.
⚠ Safety Never open a control cabinet without knowing its voltage. Even with the main disconnect off, capacitors hold charge, PLC batteries maintain memory, and incoming feeds from other cabinets may still be live. Use a meter to verify absence of voltage before touching anything.

13. Cabinet Documentation

A well-run cabinet has documentation inside the door:

  • Panel layout drawing: Shows physical location of every device in the cabinet.
  • Wiring/loop diagrams: The drawings you learned to read in F3.
  • Bill of materials: Every component with part number and manufacturer.
  • Laminated card with key information: Voltage, circuit IDs, contact information for panel builder.
  • Revision history: A log of every modification, dated and signed.
✓ Field Rule If you modify a panel, update the drawing before you leave. Redline the drawing, take a photo, and turn it in to whoever owns the drawing set. Half of all field headaches come from undocumented modifications.
04 Hands-On Exercise

Terminal Identification, Cabinet Walkthrough, Zone Mapping

Exercise A: Terminal Identification From a Photo

Find any photo of a control panel terminal strip online (search "control panel terminal blocks"). Identify:

  • What type of terminal blocks are used? (screw, spring, etc.)
  • What DIN rail is it mounted on?
  • Are there end stops at the ends of the strip?
  • Are there ground terminals (green/yellow)?
  • What jumper bars can you spot?
  • What's the labeling system — how are terminals marked?
  • Are any terminals fused or disconnect type?

Exercise B: Cabinet Layout Walkthrough

If you have access to any real control cabinet (or if you can walk through one at work), examine it against the zone layout from section 03:

  • Where are the power components (breakers, disconnects) located? Top, middle, bottom?
  • Where are the power supplies? Are they labeled?
  • Where is the PLC / control logic? Is it accessible without removing other components?
  • Where do the field wires enter? Top or bottom?
  • Where is the terminal block zone? Is it adjacent to the PLC for short wire runs, or far away?
  • Where is the earth bar? Can you see the connections?
  • What's the NEMA/IP rating? Check the label inside the door.
  • What cooling method is used? Fans, louvers, AC?
  • Does the interior temperature feel warm, hot, or cool compared to ambient?
  • Is the panel drawing inside the door? Is it current?

Exercise C: Design a Terminal Strip

Design a terminal strip on paper for the following field wiring:

  • Two 4-20 mA analog inputs (FT-101, PT-201) — 2 wires each = 4 terminals
  • Two digital inputs (LSH-301, LSL-302) — 2 wires each = 4 terminals
  • One 4-20 mA analog output (FV-101) — 2 wires = 2 terminals
  • One 24 VDC power distribution — 2 wires (24V and 0V) = 2 terminals
  • One ground terminal per cable

Include: terminal numbers, wire numbers (use the loop number as prefix), signal type grouping (analog together, digital together, power together, ground at the end), and a note about where the field wire lands vs. where the panel wire lands.

✓ Practice Goal By the end of these exercises, you should be able to walk into any control cabinet and immediately identify its zone layout, cooling method, terminal organization, and safety ratings — without needing to be shown.
