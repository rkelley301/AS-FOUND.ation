Control Valves & Final Control Elements

01 Why This Matters Now
⚡ Hook The transmitter measures. The PLC decides. But the valve is the only part of the loop that actually touches the process. When a loop won't settle, a sticky stem, a worn trim, or a positioner out of calibration is one of the first things to rule out — and no amount of tuning fixes a valve that doesn't move when it's told to. And when instrument air fails, the valve's fail position is the last safety action the loop has left.
02 The Analogy

A control valve is a door with a door-closer.

Someone holds the door partly open against a spring-loaded closer. How far it's open depends on how hard they push. The moment they let go, the closer wins — every time, in the same direction. That's a spring-and-diaphragm valve: air pushes, the spring pushes back, and when the air goes away the spring decides where the valve ends up.

✋

🔁

Always pushing back. Lose the air and it wins — that's the fail position.
03 Core Concepts

1. Anatomy of a Control Valve

A control valve assembly is two machines bolted together: a body that restricts flow, and an actuator that moves it. The positioner is the brain on the side that makes the actuator go where the controller asked.

Spring-and-diaphragm globe valve, air-to-open. The positioner compares the 4–20 mA command to the stem position it feels through the feedback linkage, and adjusts actuator air until they match.
  • Body — the pressure-containing part in the pipe. Its trim (plug, seat ring, stem) is what actually restricts flow, and what wears.
  • Bonnet and packing — where the stem leaves the body. Packing seals the process in; too tight and it adds friction that makes the stem stick.
  • Actuator — converts air pressure into stem force. In a spring-and-diaphragm actuator, air pushes one way and the spring always pushes back.
  • Positioner — a small controller of its own. It reads the stem position and adds or bleeds actuator air until the position matches the command.
  • Travel indicator — the pointer and scale on the yoke. It is the only thing that tells you where the valve actually is.

✓ Key Insight A plain I/P converter maps 4–20 mA to a fixed air range — commonly 3–15 psi. A positioner does not: it sends whatever pressure it takes to reach the commanded position. So air pressure is not position. The command, the air, and the stem are three different numbers — check all three.

4. Fail-Safe Action — ATO vs ATC

The fail position is chosen by asking one question: if the air goes away, what is the safe state for this process? Fuel to a burner should stop. Cooling water to a reactor should keep flowing.

Air-to-Open · Fail-Closed (FC)

  • Air needed to open
  • Air port below the diaphragm; spring above
  • Rising signal → valve opens
  • Air lost → spring drives it shut
  • Typical: steam, fuel gas, hazardous or pressurized fluids

Air-to-Close · Fail-Open (FO)

  • Air needed to close
  • Air port above the diaphragm; spring below
  • Rising signal → valve closes
  • Air lost → spring drives it open
  • Typical: cooling water, safety vents, pressure relief
⚠ Action alone isn't enough ATO/ATC describes the actuator's spring-and-diaphragm geometry. An installed valve's action also depends on positioner configuration, bench range, and plug orientation. Never infer fail position from the tag name alone — verify at the stem.
  • Loss of air ≠ loss of signal. Air failure is decided by the spring. Signal failure (the 4–20 mA drops out) is decided by how the positioner is configured. Know both for every valve you touch.
  • No spring, no automatic fail position. Double-acting piston actuators have no return spring. On air loss they may stay where they are ("fail in place") or drift, unless the design adds lock-up valves or a volume tank.

6. Sizing Basics — Cv

Cv is the valve's flow capacity: the US gallons per minute of 60 °F water that pass through it with a 1 psi pressure drop. For liquids:

Q (gpm) = Cv · √( ΔP (psi) / SG )

Example: a valve with Cv 10, passing water (SG 1.0) at a 16 psi drop: Q = 10 · √16 = 40 gpm.

  • Oversized — the valve does all its work in the first few % of travel. Tiny stem movements make big flow changes and the loop is hard to control, especially near closed.
  • Undersized — the valve runs wide open and still can't pass the flow the process needs. The controller output saturates at 100 %.

04 Hands-On Exercise

Part A — Valve Fail-Action Lab

Predict first, then test. The lab won't let you cut the air until you've committed to an answer and a reason.

  • Open the lab and run the guided example. Watch an ATO valve track its command up to 60 %.
  • Predict where the ATO valve goes when air is lost, and which force puts it there. Then cut the air and watch the plug move.
  • Repeat for the ATC valve — this time without having watched it move first. A wrong answer sends you back for another try at a new operating point.
  • In free exploration, cut the air and sweep the command. Watch the command and loop current keep changing while the valve position doesn't move. Command is not position.

Part B — As-Found Stroke Check (Bench or Field)

The first thing a tech does on a suspect valve: record how it's actually behaving before changing anything.

⚠ Before You Stroke Anything Stroking a valve changes the process. Only do this with operations' permission, the loop in manual, and the valve isolated or bypassed as your site's procedure requires. Keep hands clear of the yoke and stem.
  • Confirm the valve's tag, action (ATO/ATC) and fail position from the datasheet — then verify at the stem, not from the tag.
  • Put the loop in manual. Connect a meter in series with the output loop mA METER
  • Set the output to each point in the table below. Let the stem settle, then read the travel indicator.
  • Record the as-found travel before adjusting anything. Also note any point where the stem hesitated or jumped.
  • Compare the error at each point to your site's tolerance. Out of tolerance → the positioner needs calibration (M3), or the valve needs mechanical work.
