DC Circuits & Ohm's Law

01 Why This Matters Now
⚡ Hook Every 4-20 mA loop in your plant is a DC circuit. When a transmitter can't drive the signal to the controller, when a HART communicator won't communicate, when a loop reads low — the answer is almost always hiding in Ohm's law. You can't troubleshoot what you don't understand.

03 Core Concepts

1. The Three Fundamental Quantities

  • Voltage (V) — electrical potential difference. It's the cause. Without a voltage difference between two points, no current flows. Units: volts (V). Common in instrumentation: 24 VDC, 120 VAC, 5 VDC logic.
  • Current (I) — the rate of electron flow. It's the effect. Units: amperes (A). In instrumentation we live in the milliamp range: 4-20 mA is 0.004 to 0.020 A.
  • Resistance (R) — opposition to current flow. Units: ohms (Ω). Every wire, every connection, every component has some resistance. In a healthy loop, total resistance is carefully budgeted.

2. Ohm's Law — The Master Equation

Ohm's law says: the current through a conductor is directly proportional to the voltage across it, and inversely proportional to its resistance.

V = I × R

Rearrange it three ways depending on what you're solving for:

Solve ForFormulaWhen You Use It
VoltageV = I × RFind voltage drop across a known resistance at a known current
CurrentI = V / RFind loop current given supply voltage and total loop resistance
ResistanceR = V / IFind an unknown resistor value from measured V and I

3. Power — The Fourth Quantity

Power is the rate of energy consumption. It tells you how much heat a component must dissipate. Exceed it and things burn.

P = V × I  =  I²R  =  V² / R

A 250 Ω resistor with 20 mA through it dissipates P = (0.020)² × 250 = 0.1 W. A 1/4 W resistor handles that easily. A 1/8 W resistor would run hot.

4. Series Circuits

Components connected end-to-end. There is only one path for current.

  • Current is the same through every component. Itotal = I₁ = I₂ = I₃
  • Voltages add up. Vtotal = V₁ + V₂ + V₃ (Kirchhoff's Voltage Law)
  • Resistances add up. Rtotal = R₁ + R₂ + R₃

Field example: A 4-20 mA loop is a series circuit. The transmitter, the wires, the receiver resistor, and any other loads are all in series. The same 20 mA flows through every element.

5. Parallel Circuits

Components connected across the same two points. There are multiple paths for current.

  • Voltage is the same across every branch. Vtotal = V₁ = V₂ = V₃
  • Currents add up. Itotal = I₁ + I₂ + I₃ (Kirchhoff's Current Law)
  • Resistances combine reciprocally. 1/Rtotal = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + 1/R₃

Field example: Two 250 Ω resistors in parallel give 125 Ω. This matters when you're calculating loop load.

6. Kirchhoff's Laws — The Two Rules

  • KVL (Voltage Law): The sum of all voltage rises and drops around any closed loop equals zero. In plain English: whatever voltage the supply provides, the loads must consume it all.
  • KCL (Current Law): The sum of currents entering a junction equals the sum of currents leaving it. In plain English: current doesn't disappear — it goes somewhere.

Practice Problems (no calculator)

  • A 24 VDC supply drives a loop with 600 Ω total resistance. What current flows? (Answer: I = 24/600 = 0.040 A = 40 mA — but the transmitter limits it to 20 mA max. The loop is over-resisted for full scale.)
  • A 250 Ω resistor has 16 mA flowing through it. What voltage appears across it? (Answer: V = 0.016 × 250 = 4.0 V)
  • You measure 5.0 V across a 250 Ω resistor. What is the loop current? (Answer: I = 5/250 = 0.020 A = 20 mA — full scale)
  • A 250 Ω resistor is rated at 1/4 W. At 20 mA, is it safe? (Answer: P = (0.020)² × 250 = 0.1 W. Yes, comfortably safe.)
  • Two 250 Ω resistors in series. Total resistance? Current at 10 V? (Answer: 500 Ω. I = 10/500 = 0.020 A = 20 mA)
  • Two 250 Ω resistors in parallel. Total resistance? (Answer: 125 Ω)
